Poem for MarvinMercy Mercy Me

Marvin Gaye, Motown's "Prince of Soul," known for What's Going On and Mercy Mercy Me. His father, a Pentecostal minister, was violent and distant throughout his life. His father shot and killed him during an argument. Gay Sr. was later found to have a brain tumor.

Marvin sang with both hands open.
He gave it all away to the same world
that was already coming for him.
I can't do that. My hands don't open like that.

I learned them at a table.
Sundays, the long one, the dishes going around
and not once passing from a hand into a hand.
We set things down in front of each other
and stepped back and let the other one reach.
Nobody in that house ever gave anybody anything.
That is where the hands get their instructions.
Nothing was ever put in mine,
so they learned to stay shut. They are still shut.
I am telling you this with them closed.

And my father weighs nothing at all,
and I have carried him up every staircase of my life.
He is the song I know every word of
and cannot play in a room with people in it.

There were nights I built a better man out of him.
Gave him a voice that came in warm.
Gave him hands that knew the difference
between holding a boy
and holding a boy under.

Marvin's father never learned it either.
Two men who could not tell the two apart.
Two sons who found out which was which.

Marvin's ended loud, in a room, at close range.
Mine ended on a Tuesday, on the telephone,
and nobody would call it an ending.
He said the thing he always said.
I heard myself not answering.
And then I did the only violent thing
I have ever done to him: I stopped calling.
No announcement. No last word.
I let the silence in and I kept it fed.
Years of it now. I am the one who did that.

So call it settled. Call it paid.
What I have instead of a father
is a receiver, the tone going on and on,
and that exact hush before a man hangs up.
After that the line went slack the way it goes slack
between a ship and the one who walked out of it,
and neither of us has known since
whether the other is still turning out there
or has been falling a long time in the dark.

I lived inside that hush for years.
Kept the hate where you keep good silver.
Kept the loneliness turning, slow,
a record nobody dropped the needle on
that plays regardless. Some nights
the two of them lay down together
in the house where my mother was learning
to sleep on one half of a bed
and leave the other half made.

And I cursed him. Thoroughly. For years.
With all the rage one house can hold
without the windows going.

Then: they opened Marvin's father's head
and found a nameless thing
that had been growing there the whole time.
It forgave him nothing.
But it gave the living somewhere to lay the blame.

God help me, I want that for mine.
I want a nameless thing to bloom in the dark of him.
I want it to account for the Sundays.
For the Decembers when the overtime came home on time
and he did not.
For a whole house teaching itself, room by room,
not to listen for a car.

Let it be hungry. Let it go without a map.
Let it be too stupid to tell the good tissue from the bad.
No. Let it be smart.
Let it eat only the part of him that reached me,
and let it leave the man on the floor
running a toy car along the carpet,
let it leave the one who sang me to sleep
with the song about the woodpecker
who cried because he came home with empty pockets,
let it leave whoever he was
before the thing moved in behind his eyes
and started wearing him.

Something nameless. Something that lets us both put it down.

I am not Marvin.

Marvin could stand in front of everything
that wanted him dead and still sing to it
like it was worth the breath.
He had a voice that forgave the room it was in.
He had a father to be furious at,
a whole man to push against,
and even at the end there was somebody there
to do the killing.

I am soft where he was open.
Thin-skinned. I bruise at weather.
And I have no father to be furious at,
only the shape of one, only the draft
that comes in where a man was meant to stand.
Nobody sat me down. Nobody showed me the grip.
I was not taught to be a man,
I was only required to be one,
and I have been doing the impression ever since,
a boy in a coat that still does not fit,
small in the sleeves, small in the shoulders,
small in the voice that has to make the phone calls.

But listen. I want. God, I want.
I want to come out the other side of this
enormous. Not loud. Enormous.
I want to be a big man in the one way that counts,
the way Marvin is big for three minutes
in Mercy Mercy Me,
grieving the whole ruined world
with his hands open, holding nothing back,
giving it away to people who never once
passed anything from a hand into a hand.

I want to be the first one in this house
who knows the difference between holding
and holding under, and knows it in the hands,
not in the head, so my hands do it without asking.

I am not there. I am nowhere near there.
I put the needle on Mercy Mercy Me
and the crying does itself,
and somewhere under the crying
a small man is getting his growth.

CrampsCalambres

everybody's running their own trip in their head

the psychiatrist said it on the first visit:
write this down, fluoxetine,
what you've got has a name,
you're not the first

he wrote the script without asking me one question,
the way you sign a thing
you haven't read,
and there I was,
at the mercy of his hurry,
his next patient,
his not bothering to know me.

he was right about the one thing he wouldn't look at,
that what I've got has a name,
a name nobody came for,
a name left without a mouth,

so I learned to wait.
I started telling time by slow doors,
by people who walk in and don't walk back out the same,
by the gap between a name
and what a name won't say.

something came back one night
reeking of liquor, of having swallowed the city,
it had my last name on it,
and a face like a map
of everything Mexico City does to you
once you take the appetite in your arms.
maniacal maneuver

like light in love with losing its way,
some days I can't tell if I'm the one walking
or the one watching himself walk
a second late,

and some days I'm just holding out for a Sunday,
one stupid Sunday to talk me down.

a splinter in my finger
I let it go
it digs
it finds the vein
the room closes in
and the grooves in my fingertips
come up like
walls
and start eating[ hey, your feet, the floor ]
[ breathe, come back ]

I know what it is when the whole thing gets away from you.
Not from outside. From in here.

for this wrecked body,
for this paper mirror
that soaks through before it tears,
I apologize for taking up the room
before anybody asks.

but some days I think I work,
not well,
the way a botched thing still works,
like a scribble that from across the room reads as a face,
like a puzzle I forced together,
crooked, but all of it there,

and even so,
here I am.
Beaten,
and knowing I'm beaten,
which isn't the same thing.

What HurtsLo que duele

What hurts has a name now.
It keeps a time: three in the morning.
It keeps a place: the cold half of the bed.
It has somebody to blame,
and blaming him fixes nothing.

Now it's the bone that won't give that hurts,
the body still standing guard
over something that ended and never left.

What hurts has a prescription now,
twenty milligrams, forty when twenty won't hold,
and named, and filled,
it hurts exactly the same.

What hurts gets as close in
as being afraid to sleep alone
in a house that has learned to keep quiet.
