Fourteen Years Apart2011 — 2025

Four poems written at twenty-two. Four answers written fourteen years later. The same voice, twice.

In 2011, at twenty-two, I wrote four poems under the name El Zhurdo. They won a competition. I hadn't written them to win.

In 2025 I read them again. Four answers came out, unplanned. They aren't corrections. They're what the same person would say fourteen years later, in a different body and the same language.

I

The only 2011 poem that needed no revising. Its answer is the longest thing in the file.

2011

Larva

Begin already naked.
Don't die inside your shelter.
Don't feel what we believe is foreign.
Don't exist without the world,
even if the world exists without you.

To feel ourselves distant from a tear
that plants manias of love between fits of laughter,
manias endless, lit
even in the most shuttered eyes.

We are like rivers that don't know
they're headed for the sea,
that don't know that loving
wasn't waiting for them either.

We walk stumbling, in a hurry,
on hunches that ask no permission
to lose themselves in another language,
one that doesn't know your name yet,
we walk,
feeling the warmth of your eyes
in mine.

Cramps in the SoulCalambres en el alma

everyone's carrying their own trip in their head

the psychiatrist said on the first visit:
write it down: fluoxetine,
what you feel has a name,
and you're not the first

he prescribed it without asking me anything,
like someone signing something
without reading what they sign,
and there I was,
at the mercy of his rush,
his next patient,
his laziness about knowing me.

he was right about the one thing he refused to know,
that what I have has a name,
a name nobody claimed,
that was left without a mouth,

and so I learned to wait,
measuring time by doors that take too long,
by people who come in and don't come back the same,
by the exact distance between a name
and what a name sometimes withholds.

something came back one night
smelling of alcohol and of swallowing the city whole,
it carried my last name,
and a face that was a map
of everything Mexico City does
when you embrace the demons of desire.
maniacal maneuver

like light that adores its own straying,
sometimes I don't know if I'm the one walking
or someone watching himself live
a second after living it,

and sometimes I just wait for a Sunday,
one stupid Sunday to calm me down.

a splinter in my finger
I ignore it
it digs in
it reaches the veins
the world shrinks
just the grooves in the skin of my fingers
turning into enormous
walls
eating me[ hey, your feet, the floor ]
[ breathe, come back ]

I know exactly what it feels like when everything spins out of control.
Not from the outside. From the inside.

for this ruined body,
for this paper mirror
that goes damp before it tears,
I'm sorry for being here
before anyone asks me to.

but sometimes I think I can function,
not well,

Poem for CarringtonPoema a Carrington

At the start of this account there was a feeling,
again, and there was a topology of fear,
of open tears, the x-ray of wind
leaving its flesh, fear turning into form.

Because of the painting of the raging bull,
the beast that was a girl on a bicycle
and at the same time was her vast, infinite lover.

After all, the dream is sick,
its shoulders loose, the brushstrokes of the wind's bride;
wings.

According to Oskar, his disorder needs a homomorphism;
though Lucrecio argues for the poem's own destruction:
the fact that the author himself becomes the poem,
existing only in his poetry, nothing more than his voice.

Poem for MarvinMercy Mercy Me

Marvin Gaye (1939–1984), Motown's "Prince of Soul," known for What's Going On and Mercy Mercy Me. His father, a Pentecostal minister, was violent and distant throughout his life. On April 1, 1984, one day before Marvin's 45th birthday, his father shot and killed him during an argument. Gay Sr. was later found to have a brain tumor.

I'd want to offer you my heart with an open hand
wanting nothing back,
the way Marvin sang to the world that was destroying him.

But I carry things.

My father weighs in a way you can't see,
like a song you know
but can't play at any party.

I hid him in the silence of Sundays,
at the table where no one passed anything hand to hand.

There was an imagined time
where his voice didn't arrive cold,
where the father's hands knew the difference
between holding and drowning.

Marvin's father's hands didn't know it either.

So we're even.
That's all.
Like astronauts who lost the line
and no longer know if the other is still there
or has spent years drifting alone in the dark.

There was no goodbye.
There was a telephone, its long dial tone,
the exact silence before hanging up.

I stayed in that silence a long time,
with the hatred well kept,
with the loneliness turning slowly
like a record no one put on but that plays anyway,
and then both things together
sleeping in a house where my mother
learned to sleep in half a bed.

I cursed him.
For years, in detail,
with all the rage that fits in that same house.

They found something growing nameless
in Marvin's father's head.
That didn't absolve him, but there was something to point to.

Sometimes I wish that for mine,
something that grows nameless in the dark,
that explains the Sundays without him,
the Christmases where the work arrived on time and he didn't,
the whole house learning not to wait for him.

Something that eats whatever it finds,
that moves without a map, that can't tell healthy from sick,
that eats what hurt me
and leaves the other one intact,
the one who pushed toy cars along the floor,
the one who sang about the woodpecker
who cried because he brought no money,
the father who existed
before something came to live inside him
that wasn't entirely him.

Something nameless that frees us both.

I'm not Marvin,
but I put on Mercy Mercy Me
and I cry.

What HurtsLo que duele

What hurts isn't the chest,
isn't hunger, nor the open wound,
not the muscles, nor the body
silencing its own language.

The bone that gives doesn't hurt,
the edge of time
entering uninvited doesn't hurt,
what bleeds doesn't hurt.

What hurts has no name in the body
but lives there,
stitched to the ribs,
keeping the rhythm without being asked.

What hurts runs as deep
as the quiet pleasure of being naked.

What HurtsLo que duele

What hurts does have a name.
It has a time: three in the morning.
It has a place: the empty side of the bed.
It has someone to blame,
though blame relieves nothing.

The bone that learned not to give in hurts,
the body that keeps watch
over what already passed but never left.

What hurts comes with a prescription,
twenty milligrams, forty if that's not enough,
and even named, even prescribed,
it hurts just the same.

What hurts is as intimate
as the fear of sleeping alone
in a house that learned to go quiet.
